About

I'm a PhD candidate in Chemical & Biomolecular Engineering at Georgia Tech, advised by Prof. Anant K. Paravastu.

Alzheimer's amyloid-β42 can assemble into ordered fibrils or into small, soluble oligomers. My thesis, Controlling Alzheimer's Amyloid-β42 Oligomer Formation by Pathway Selection, asks what decides between these two outcomes and how the decision can be changed on purpose. I use solid-state NMR, cryo-EM and biophysical chromatography alongside all-atom and coarse-grained simulation. With the Wood lab, I connect oligomer structure to how microglia respond to it.

Before graduate school I spent four years in industry: first as a process engineer at Manali Petrochemicals, then as a data analyst on Amazon's Alexa Shopping team. That work taught me to build reproducible, quantitative workflows, and I bring the same habits to the lab.

Research

Thesis · in progress

Controlling Alzheimer's amyloid-β42 oligomer formation by pathway selection

Amyloid-β42 has two main ways to assemble. It can form long, ordered fibrils, or it can form small, soluble oligomers, which are increasingly linked to toxicity in Alzheimer's disease. My work looks for the molecular features that send the peptide down one path or the other, then uses them as levers to change which path it takes.

01

Oligomers for structural biology

Synthetic Aβ42 races toward fibrils unless it is steered. I developed a protocol for preparing Aβ42 oligomers in the quantity and quality that solid-state NMR needs: removing fibril seeds, directing monomers into oligomers with detergent micelles, then separating and cleaning them up by size-exclusion chromatography and dialysis.

02

Deamidation as a lever

Deamidation at Q15 or N27 swaps a neutral amide side chain for a charged acid. With the Wood and Raskatov labs, we showed that this redirects Aβ42 aggregation, reduces soluble oligomer formation and toxicity, and lowers microglial inflammatory cytokines and CD68.

03

Designer peptide assemblies

I combine solid-state NMR and cryo-EM on cross-α peptide filaments built by reprogramming coiled-coil design rules, and contribute to collaborative work on redox-tunable condensates and angiogenic nanofiber hydrogels.
