Structural biology & peptide design

How peptides self-assemble, and how to design them to do it.

We use solid-state NMR and computational modeling to resolve the molecular structures of peptide assemblies, from the amyloid-β oligomers implicated in Alzheimer's disease to designer nanofibers for biomaterials.

Research

Three lines of inquiry

Many proteins can take more than one aggregation pathway, and each pathway ends in a different structure. We work out those structures one atom at a time, using isotope-labeled peptides and solid-state NMR paired with simulation.

01 · DISEASE

Alzheimer's amyloid-β oligomers

Growing evidence points to small, soluble oligomers, not plaques, as the main toxic species in Alzheimer's disease. We solve the structure of stable ~150 kDa Aβ42 oligomers to learn why they stop growing and how they damage cells.

Read more

Aβ oligomers can form on-pathway, eventually becoming fibrils, or off-pathway, staying size-limited. Our collaborators at the Mayo Clinic prepare stable off-pathway oligomers of uniform size, built from roughly 50 peptide strands. Solid-state NMR shows that these oligomers hold out-of-register parallel β-sheets alongside antiparallel β-sheets, which sets them apart from fibrils.

With Dr. Terrone Rosenberry (Mayo Clinic)
02 · MISFOLDING

Misfolded proteins: myocilin & glaucoma

Mutations in the eye protein myocilin make it prone to forming amyloid in some forms of glaucoma. We determine the molecular structure of these fibrils, from short peptide fragments up to the full-length protein.

Read more

Myocilin forms two distinct fibril shapes, straight and circular, depending on assembly conditions. Two peptide segments, P1 and P3, reproduce these two shapes on their own. Understanding what drives each structure could guide therapies for the disease.

With Dr. Raquel Lieberman (Georgia Tech)
03 · DESIGN

Designer peptide assemblies

De novo peptides that form β-sheet and coiled-coil nanofibers show promise for drug delivery, immunoengineering and regenerative medicine. We map sequence to structure to guide the next generation of designs.

Read more

Self-assembling β-sheet peptides

Alternating hydrophobic and hydrophilic residues drives peptides such as RADA16-I into β-sheet nanofibers. We are also closing the loop between solid-state NMR and computational design to search sequence space more efficiently.

Coassembling coiled-coil peptides

In the two-component SAF-p1/p2a system, solid-state NMR revealed an unexpected temperature-driven change from α-helix to β-sheet. We now ask how to design that transition on purpose.

Coassembling β-sheet peptides

Charge-complementary peptide pairs were assumed to alternate perfectly along the sheet, but NMR and discontinuous molecular dynamics simulations show many self-paired strands. We combine both approaches to design better coassembling pairs.

With Dr. Carol Hall (NC State) and Dr. Gregory Hudalla (UF)

People

The group

Dr. Anant Paravastu

Principal Investigator
  • Ph.D., Chemical Engineering, University of California, Berkeley (2004)
  • B.S., Chemical Engineering, Massachusetts Institute of Technology (1998)

Anant's Ph.D. work with Jeffrey Reimer used lasers to control nuclear spin polarization in GaAs. As a postdoc with Robert Tycko at the NIH Laboratory of Chemical Physics (2004–2007), he turned NMR to structural biology. He was part of the community that showed a single amyloid peptide can follow several aggregation pathways to several distinct structures. He was on the faculty at Florida State University and the National High Magnetic Field Laboratory from 2008 to 2015, then joined Georgia Tech.
